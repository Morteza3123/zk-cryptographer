That closes out phase two, polynomials. **Phase three starts here: groups, curves, and pairings** — the foundation everything from elliptic curves to bilinear pairings gets built on. Today stays simple: what is a group, and what makes one *cyclic*?

## What is a group?

A group is a much lighter structure than the finite fields built back in the finite fields lesson. It strips things down to the bare minimum that's still useful.

<MathBox label="Group vs. field">

A **field** needs all four operations — add, subtract, multiply, divide. A **group** needs only a set and *one* operation, plus a short list of rules that operation has to follow.

</MathBox>

## The four group rules

Those rules have a name: the **group axioms**. Every one of them has to hold for a set and an operation to count as a group.

## What makes a group cyclic?

Some groups have a special property: **every element can be reached by repeatedly applying the operation to just one starting element**. Starting from $1$ and adding $1$ over and over, under addition mod 7, visits every element in the group before landing back on the start.

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — C₇, the cyclic group of order 7. Starting from 1 and following the arrows reaches every element before wrapping back around.">
  <CyclicGroupRingFigure />
</Figure>

An element that does this — reaches the whole group on its own — is called a **generator**.

## Generating the group from one element

Watching the running total confirms it directly: adding $1$ to itself, again and again, counts straight through every element of the group.

<Figure caption="Fig. 4 — the running total of 1 + 1 + 1 + ... mod 7, landing on every element before wrapping back to the start.">
  <GeneratingGroupFigure />
</Figure>

Seven additions of $1$ visit every element of $C_7$ exactly once, then land back on the identity — $1$ really does generate the whole group.

## Why this matters for ZK

The elliptic curve groups used throughout ZK cryptography are built on this exact same cyclic structure — just with **points on a curve** standing in for numbers on a ring.

<Figure caption="Fig. 5 — the same one-way gap as C₇'s generator, scaled up to a curve: easy forward, hard backward.">
  <DiscreteLogFigure />
</Figure>

There's a generator point $G$, and for a scalar $K$, computing $K \cdot G$ is easy. Going the other direction — recovering $K$ from $K \cdot G$ — is hard. **That one-way difficulty is the discrete log problem**, and it's what every elliptic curve scheme in this series will lean on from here forward.
