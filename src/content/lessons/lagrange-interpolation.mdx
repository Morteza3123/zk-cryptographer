Every lesson so far started with a polynomial and worked outward from it — building, evaluating, combining. **Today flips the problem around**: given a handful of points, how do you find the one polynomial that passes through all of them? That question has a name, Lagrange interpolation, and it's the exact mechanism that turns an entire ZK circuit into a single polynomial.

## The problem

Take three points: $(0, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, and $(2, 5)$. What polynomial passes through all three? With exactly three points, exactly one polynomial of degree $2$ fits.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — three points, and the one degree-2 polynomial that passes through all of them.">
  <LagrangeCurveFigure />
</Figure>

## The trick: basis polynomials

The trick is to build one small helper polynomial per point — a **Lagrange basis polynomial**. Each one is engineered to equal $1$ at its own point and $0$ at every other point in the set.

<MathBox label="A Lagrange basis polynomial">

$$L_i(x) = 1 \text{ at its own point}, \quad L_i(x) = 0 \text{ at every other point}$$

Each basis polynomial only ever "turns on" for its own point — everywhere else, it contributes nothing.

</MathBox>

Building one is mechanical: for every *other* point, multiply in a factor of $(x - \text{that point})$, then divide by whatever that same product evaluates to at your own point — which forces the result to land on exactly $1$ there.

<Figure caption="Fig. 2 — the three basis polynomials for x = 0, 1, 2. Each numerator zeroes out at every point but its own.">
  <LagrangeBasisFigure />
</Figure>

## Combining them

With all three basis polynomials built, combine them by scaling each one by its point's $y$-value and adding the results together.

<MathBox label="The combined polynomial">

$$P(x) = 1 \cdot L_0(x) + 2 \cdot L_1(x) + 5 \cdot L_2(x)$$

</MathBox>

Checking this against the original points confirms it works — at each $x$, exactly one basis polynomial is "on," and it hands back exactly that point's $y$-value.

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — checking the combined polynomial at each of the three points. Only one basis polynomial ever turns on at a time.">
  <LagrangeCheckFigure />
</Figure>

Expanded out, all of that simplifies down to a single clean expression: $P(x) = x^2 + 1$ — exactly the curve from Fig. 1.

## The general formula

Nothing about this is specific to three points. The same construction works for any number of them:

<MathBox label="Lagrange interpolation, in general">

$$P(x) = \sum_i y_i \cdot L_i(x)$$

Sum each point's $y$-value times its own basis polynomial — that's the entire formula, however many points there are.

</MathBox>

## Why this matters for ZK

Here's the payoff. In a **QAP** — the structure behind Groth16 — every gate in a circuit becomes a point: the gate's index paired with its value.

<Figure caption="Fig. 4 — Lagrange interpolation turns every gate-as-a-point into one polynomial representing the whole circuit.">
  <LagrangeQAPFigure />
</Figure>

Lagrange interpolation takes that entire set of points and produces a single polynomial representing the whole circuit at once. **This is literally what the Q in QAP stands for** — Quadratic Arithmetic Program — the same idea this lesson just built from scratch, running at the scale of a real circuit.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Lagrange interpolation finds the one polynomial that passes through a given set of points.
- A Lagrange basis polynomial $L_i(x)$ equals $1$ at its own point and $0$ at every other point in the set.
- The combined polynomial is built by scaling each basis polynomial by its point's $y$-value and summing them: $P(x) = \sum_i y_i \cdot L_i(x)$.
- The construction works for any number of points, not just the three in this lesson's worked example.
- In a QAP, every gate in a circuit becomes a point, and Lagrange interpolation turns that whole set into one polynomial representing the circuit — literally the "Q" in QAP.

</KeyTakeaways>
