The last lesson built elliptic curves: point addition, and scalar multiplication. **Today builds the operation that makes Groth16 verification and KZG commitments actually work — the bilinear pairing.** It's a strange operation that takes two points and connects two completely separate worlds.

## The big picture

A pairing is best understood as a bridge between two separate elliptic curve groups, landing somewhere entirely different on the other side.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — P lives in G₁, Q lives in G₂, two separate elliptic curve groups. The pairing e(P, Q) bridges them into a third group, Gₜ, underneath.">
  <PairingBridgeFigure />
</Figure>

It's worth being precise about which group each piece actually belongs to, since $P$, $Q$, and the result are all different kinds of object: $P$ is a point in $G_1$, $Q$ is a point in $G_2$, and the output $e(P, Q)$ lands in a completely different group, $G_T$.

<MathBox label="What Gₜ actually is">

The output of a pairing is **not** a point on either curve — it's a different kind of object entirely. $G_T$ is the multiplicative group of a finite field: $e(P, Q)$ is a *number*, not a point.

</MathBox>

## What "bilinear" means

The word *bilinear* refers to one very specific, very useful algebraic property this operation has.

<MathBox label="Bilinearity">

$$e(a \cdot P,\ b \cdot Q) = e(P, Q)^{ab}$$

Scalars multiplied **inside** the pairing come back out as an **exponent on the outside**.

</MathBox>

## Why this matters for ZK

Here's the payoff: the bilinear property lets a verifier confirm a hidden multiplication happened correctly, without ever learning the actual numbers involved.

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — checking e(A, B) = e(C, D) confirms a·b = c·d, without the verifier ever seeing a, b, c, or d.">
  <PairingVerificationFigure />
</Figure>

That single check — one pairing equality, standing in for a multiplication the verifier never actually sees — **is how Groth16 and KZG verify a proof**.
