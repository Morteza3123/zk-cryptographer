The last lesson showed a hash replacing a live verifier with a single computation. **This lesson tackles a different problem entirely: how does someone lock in a value now, prove later they never changed it, while keeping that value completely secret the whole time in between?** That's a commitment scheme — the second building block, alongside hashing, that almost every ZK proof system is built from.

## The core idea: commit, then reveal

Think of a sealed envelope. Write something down, seal it, hand it over — nobody can see what's inside, but it also can't be swapped for a different piece of paper later. A commitment scheme does exactly that with numbers instead of paper.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — commit now, locked and sealed. Reveal later, and the receiver can check it against what was sent.">
  <CommitRevealFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="A commitment scheme">

Two phases: $C = \text{Commit}(m, r)$ sends only $C$ — the receiver learns nothing about $m$ yet. Later, revealing $m$ and the opening key $r$ lets the receiver check that they really do produce that same $C$.

</MathBox>

## Two promises: hiding and binding

That envelope metaphor only holds together if two separate promises are both kept at once. Break either one, and the scheme is useless.

<Figure caption="Fig. 2 — hiding: C alone could belong to any message. Binding: once C is sent, it can never be reopened to a different one.">
  <HidingBindingFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Hiding and binding">

**Hiding** — $C$ on its own reveals nothing about $m$; every possible message stays equally plausible. **Binding** — the committer can never produce a valid opening of $C$ to a message other than the one they actually started with.

</MathBox>

## A real construction — the Pedersen commitment

So far this has all been conceptual. Here's an actual working construction, built entirely from tools already in this series: elliptic curve points, from topic 12.

<MathBox label="The Pedersen commitment">

$$C = g \cdot m + h \cdot r$$

$g$ and $h$ are two fixed generator points — both public, known to everyone. $m$ is the secret message being committed to. $r$ is a random **blinding factor**, chosen fresh and kept secret too.

</MathBox>

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — one curve point, two secrets baked in. Pulling m or r back out of C is exactly the discrete log problem from topic 11.">
  <PedersenCommitmentFigure />
</Figure>

## Why the blinding factor matters

That $r$ in the formula might look like an unnecessary extra ingredient. It isn't — it's the entire reason hiding actually works. Watch what happens committing to the exact same message three separate times, with three different random $r$ values.

<Figure caption="Fig. 4 — same m = 42 every time. Three different r values produce three completely unrelated-looking commitments.">
  <BlindingFactorFigure />
</Figure>

Without $r$, committing to the same value twice would produce the exact same $C$ both times — and that repetition alone would leak the fact that the hidden values matched.

## KZG — committing to an entire polynomial

A Pedersen commitment locks in a single number. **KZG does something far more powerful: it commits to an entire polynomial** — the exact object this series spent its second phase building — and compresses it down to one point.

<Figure caption="Fig. 5 — a polynomial with thousands of terms compresses to one curve point. Opening it at a random location proves a value without revealing the rest.">
  <KZGCommitmentFigure />
</Figure>

The verifier picks a random evaluation point $x = a$; the prover reveals $y = p(a)$ along with a short proof $\pi$; the verifier checks a pairing equation — the exact tool built in topic 13. **This is what a Groth16 or PLONK proof actually sends: not the polynomial, just this.**

## Why this matters for ZK

Here's how commitments and the Fiat-Shamir transform from the last lesson fit together into one system — and why the order of operations is the whole point.

<Figure caption="Fig. 6 — commitments lock in first. Only then does the challenge exist. There's no path back to change what was already sealed.">
  <CommitOrderFigure />
</Figure>

The prover commits to their polynomials **first** — those are sealed by binding. Only *then* does the random challenge get generated, as $c = H(\text{commitments})$, straight from the Fiat-Shamir transform built last lesson. **Commit before seeing the question.** This exact ordering — commit, then challenge — is what stops a prover from cheating in Groth16, PLONK, and KZG alike.
