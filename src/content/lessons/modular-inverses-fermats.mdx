The last lesson covered addition, subtraction, and multiplication inside a modulus, and quietly skipped the fourth operation. **That gap gets fixed here** — division turns out to be the one operation that doesn't survive the trip into modular arithmetic unchanged, and patching it back in is what this lesson is about.

## Why division breaks

In ordinary arithmetic, dividing by a number is identical to multiplying by its reciprocal: $6 \div 2$ and $6 \times \tfrac{1}{2}$ land on the same $3$. The trouble is that a modulus only has whole numbers to work with — there's no slot in $\{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ for a fraction like $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to occupy.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — the same division, rewritten as a reciprocal, breaks the moment it's asked to live inside mod 7.">
  <ModularDivisionFigure />
</Figure>

What stands in for the reciprocal instead is called a **modular inverse**: a whole number that, multiplied by the original, lands on exactly $1$.

<MathBox label="Modular inverse">

$$a \times x \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$$

$x$ is the **modular inverse** of $a$ — the number that undoes multiplying by $a$, the same job a reciprocal does everywhere else.

</MathBox>

## Finding one by brute force

For small numbers, there's nothing cleverer to do than try every candidate. Take $3 \bmod 7$: run $x = 1$ through $x = 5$ and check which one lands on $1$.

<Figure caption="Fig. 2 — checking 3 × x against every x from 1 to 5. x = 5 is the first one that lands on 1.">
  <ModularInverseSearchFigure a={3} n={7} rows={[1, 2, 3, 4, 5]} matchIndex={4} />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Verifying the result">

$$3 \times 5 = 15, \quad 15 \bmod 7 = 1 \implies 3^{-1} \equiv 5 \pmod{7}$$

</MathBox>

## Why not every number has one

Brute force doesn't always end in success. Try the same search for $2 \bmod 6$:

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — every x from 1 to 5, checked against 2 × x ≡ 1 (mod 6). None of them land on 1.">
  <ModularInverseSearchFigure a={2} n={6} rows={[1, 2, 3, 4, 5]} footer="no x ever gives 1 — 2 has no inverse mod 6, because gcd(2, 6) = 2, not 1" />
</Figure>

No candidate works, and the reason is a single number: the **greatest common divisor** of $a$ and $n$.

<MathBox label="When an inverse exists">

$$\gcd(a, n) = 1$$

An inverse exists only when $a$ and $n$ share no common factor besides $1$. $\gcd(2, 6) = 2$, which is exactly why the search above never found one. It's also why **ZK systems build their modulus out of a prime number**: every value from $1$ to $p-1$ automatically shares no factor with a prime $p$, so every one of them is guaranteed an inverse.

</MathBox>

## Fermat's little theorem: a shortcut for huge primes

Brute force works fine for $6 \bmod 7$, and falls apart completely once the modulus is a 254-bit prime — no computer is checking that many candidates one at a time. **Fermat's little theorem** is the shortcut that replaces the search with a single formula.

<MathBox label="Fermat's little theorem">

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$$

Holds whenever $p$ is prime and $a$ isn't a multiple of $p$ — true for every number from $1$ to $p-1$, no exceptions.

</MathBox>

Three lines turn that statement into a formula for the inverse itself, just by splitting the exponent apart and lining the result up against the definition from the start of this lesson:

<Figure caption="Fig. 4 — splitting Fermat's exponent in two turns the theorem directly into the inverse formula.">
  <FermatDerivationFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="The inverse formula">

$$a^{-1} \equiv a^{p-2} \pmod{p}$$

Raise $a$ to the power of $p - 2$, reduce by $p$, and whatever comes out **is** the inverse — no search required.

</MathBox>

## Checking the formula against the brute-force answer

The whole point of a formula is that it has to agree with the slow method it's replacing. Plug $a = 3$, $p = 7$ back in:

<MathBox label="Worked example">

$$
\begin{aligned}
3^{-1} &\equiv 3^{7-2} \pmod{7} \\
&\equiv 3^5 \pmod{7} \\
&\equiv 243 \bmod 7 \\
&\equiv 5 \pmod{7}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Matches** the brute-force answer from a few sections back exactly — $5$, both times.

</MathBox>

## Why Circom secretly computes this

There's no division gate inside a finite field. A Circom circuit can't compute $a \div b \pmod{p}$ directly, because — same as everywhere else in this lesson — that operation doesn't exist there. What it computes instead is $a$ times $b$'s modular inverse.

<Figure caption="Fig. 5 — every division written in a Circom circuit compiles down to a multiplication by a modular inverse.">
  <CircuitDivisionFigure />
</Figure>

Every constraint in a circuit that looks like division is, underneath, running exactly the formula from this lesson. The next lesson gives this whole structure — a modulus, closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and now division too — its proper name: a **finite field**.
