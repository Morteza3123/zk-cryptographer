This one looks almost too simple to deserve its own lesson. It isn't. **Every cryptographic protocol and every ZK circuit, underneath all the theory, is a pile of variables tied together with equations** — look inside any real circuit and that's all you'll find: named values and the constraints that relate them. Modular arithmetic, finite fields, polynomial commitments — all of it is this same idea, just dressed up. So before any of that, it's worth being precise about what a variable, an expression, and an equation actually are.

## Variables — a name for a value

A variable is a symbol standing in for a value. Pick a letter, usually $x$, $y$, or $z$, and let it hold something:

<MathBox label="Example">

$$x = 5$$

$x$ is the variable, $5$ is what it currently holds.

</MathBox>

It helps to picture a variable as a **labeled box**: the label is its name, and whatever's sitting inside is its value. `age = 25` is a box named "age" with `25` inside it — exactly the same idea a programming language uses when you write `age = 25` in Python or JavaScript.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — a variable is a labeled box. Nothing stops that box from staying closed.">
  <VariableBoxFigure />
</Figure>

That second box is the one cryptography actually cares about. Say there's a password, or any value someone wants to keep private — call it `secret`. Writing `secret = 12345` and then only ever referring to `secret` means the value itself never has to appear again. The box still exists, it's still a variable, it just isn't opened. That single habit — naming a value instead of writing it out — is the seed that **zero-knowledge proofs grow from**: proving something about a box without ever lifting the lid.

## Expressions — a calculation, not yet a claim

An expression combines values, variables, and operations into a single computed quantity. The giveaway is that it has **no equals sign** — it isn't asserting anything, it's just describing a calculation:

<MathBox label="Example">

$$x + 5 \qquad 2x \qquad x^2 + 3$$

Three expressions. $2x$ is shorthand for $2 \cdot x$ — in algebra, a number written directly next to a variable always means multiplication.

</MathBox>

Expressions are where the actual work happens in a cryptographic system. If $x$ is a secret value, the expression $x^2$ means "take whatever is in that box and multiply it by itself" — and a surprising amount of real cryptography is exactly this: running a calculation over a hidden value without ever needing to see what's inside.

## Equations — a claim that two things are equal

An equation goes one step further than an expression: it states that two expressions are equal.

<MathBox label="Example">

$$x + 5 = 12$$

Whatever $x$ is, adding $5$ to it produces $12$.

</MathBox>

<Figure caption="Fig. 2 — an equation is a balance: whatever sits on each side, the claim is that they weigh the same.">
  <EquationBalanceFigure left="x + 5" right="12" />
</Figure>

Solving it means isolating $x$ — doing the same operation to both sides until $x$ stands alone:

<MathBox label="Solving">

$$
\begin{aligned}
x + 5 &= 12 \\
x + 5 - 5 &= 12 - 5 \\
x &= 7
\end{aligned}
$$

Subtracting $5$ from both sides keeps the balance intact while peeling the $5$ away from $x$.

</MathBox>

And it's checkable — plug the answer back in and confirm both sides still agree:

<MathBox label="Verifying">

$$7 + 5 = 12 \quad \checkmark$$

</MathBox>

This solve-then-verify pattern shows up constantly in proof systems: a prover produces a value, and the whole protocol exists to let someone else check it satisfies the equation, without redoing the work of finding it.

## One equation, two variables

Equations don't have to pin a variable down to a single number — they can describe a relationship between two variables instead:

<MathBox label="Example">

$$y = x + 10$$

If $x = 5$, then $y = 5 + 10 = 15$.

</MathBox>

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — a value goes in, the equation acts on it, a second variable comes out.">
  <FunctionRelationFigure />
</Figure>

That's a complete constraint: feed in $x$, the equation determines $y$. A real ZK circuit is built from a long chain of exactly these — thousands or millions of small relationships like this one, linked so that the output of one feeds the input of the next.

## Back to the box that stays closed

Pull these three ideas together and the earlier example from this track's introduction stops being a curiosity and starts being a template. Someone claims to know a value $x$ such that:

$$x \cdot x = 25$$

That's an equation. $x$ is a variable — the box that stays shut. $x \cdot x$ is the expression describing what gets checked. A zero-knowledge proof is a way to convince someone this equation holds **for some value inside that box, without ever opening it**. Strip away the cryptography and what's left is exactly what this lesson covered: a named value, a calculation, and a claim of equality. Everything from here — modular arithmetic, finite fields, polynomial constraints — is this same shape, scaled up.
