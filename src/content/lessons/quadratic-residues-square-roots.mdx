Last time added subgroups, cosets, and roots of unity. **This lesson asks a question those tools didn't answer: inside a finite field, does every number have a square root?** In ordinary math, yes, always. In a finite field, no — some numbers have two square roots and some have none at all, and ZK systems need to know which is which. One small example carries the whole lesson: the prime $13$.

## Which numbers are squares?

Square every nonzero number from $1$ to $12$ mod $13$, and a pattern shows up immediately: every result appears exactly twice.

<Figure caption="Fig. 1 — squaring every number from 1 to 12 mod 13. Each result appears twice, because x and 13 − x always give the same square, leaving six squares and six non-squares.">
  <QuadraticResiduesTableFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Quadratic residues">

That symmetry isn't a coincidence: $13 - x$ is the same as $-x$ mod $13$, and $(-x)^2$ is just $x^2$. A number that is the square of something is called a **quadratic residue**. Here, exactly six of the twelve nonzero numbers are — half.

</MathBox>

## Squares are the even powers

Topic 19 built this group from a generator, $2$. Looking at squares through that lens explains *why* it's exactly half, not just confirms it.

<Figure caption="Fig. 2 — the powers of 2 mod 13, colored by whether the exponent is even or odd. The even powers are exactly the squares from Fig. 1, and the odd powers are exactly the non-squares.">
  <SquaresAsEvenPowersFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Squares as even powers">

<div slot="formula">

$$(g^j)^2 = g^{2j}$$

</div>

Squaring any power of the generator doubles its exponent, landing on an even power every time. So every square is an even power of $g$, and every even power is a square — splitting the group's twelve elements into six even-power squares and six odd-power non-squares.

</MathBox>

## The square test

Building a full table doesn't scale to a 254-bit prime. One exponentiation does the same job in a single step.

<Figure caption="Fig. 3 — the square test worked through four values mod 13: raising a to (p − 1)/2 gives 1 for a square and −1 for a non-square, matching the table from Fig. 1 exactly.">
  <LegendreSquareTestFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="The square test">

<div slot="formula">

$$a^{(p-1)/2} \equiv 1 \pmod p \text{ if } a \text{ is a square, and } -1 \text{ otherwise}$$

</div>

Write $a$ as $g^k$ for the generator $g$. Then $a^{(p-1)/2} = g^{k(p-1)/2}$, and the last section showed this collapses to $1$ when $k$ is even and $-1$ when $k$ is odd — exactly the square / non-square split. This quantity is called the **Legendre symbol**, written $\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)$.

</MathBox>

## An easy square root

The test says whether a root exists. Finding the root itself is just as fast — when the prime has the right shape.

<Figure caption="Fig. 4 — finding √3 mod 11 in one exponentiation, since 11 leaves remainder 3 when divided by 4. Both roots check out, and a short derivation shows why the formula works.">
  <EasySquareRootFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="A square root, when p ≡ 3 (mod 4)">

<div slot="formula">

$$\sqrt{a} \equiv a^{(p+1)/4} \pmod p$$

</div>

Squaring this candidate gives $a^{(p+1)/2} = a \cdot a^{(p-1)/2}$, and the square test says that last factor is $1$ whenever $a$ is a square — so the square of the candidate really is $a$. This only works when $p$ leaves remainder $3$ mod $4$; $13$ leaves remainder $1$, so $13$ needs a different tool.

</MathBox>

## When the shortcut fails

For a prime like $13$, the easy formula doesn't apply. The Tonelli–Shanks algorithm handles every prime, by maintaining a guess $R$ and an error term $t$, and fixing the guess until the error disappears.

<Figure caption="Fig. 5 — finding √10 mod 13 with Tonelli–Shanks. R is a running guess satisfying R² = 10 × t; one round of fixing the guess drives the error t to 1, which means R is exact.">
  <TonelliShanksFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Tonelli–Shanks">

<div slot="formula">

$$R^2 \equiv a \cdot t \pmod p, \qquad \text{fix } R \text{ and } t \text{ together until } t \equiv 1$$

</div>

Split $p - 1 = Q \cdot 2^S$, pick any non-square $z$ as a lever, and start from $R = a^{(Q+1)/2}$ and $t = a^Q$. Each round measures how many squarings turn $t$ into $1$, then multiplies $R$ and $t$ by a correction built from $z$ that cancels exactly that much error. When $t$ finally equals $1$, $R^2 = a$, and $R$ is the square root.

</MathBox>

## One extra bit instead of y

Here's the first payoff. A curve point has two coordinates, but $x$ almost determines $y$ on its own — only the sign is missing, and a square root recovers it.

<Figure caption="Fig. 6 — compressing the point (3, 11) on y² = x³ + 3 mod 13 down to x and a single parity bit, then rebuilding y with a square root and that bit.">
  <PointCompressionFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="Point compression">

<div slot="formula">

$$y = \pm\sqrt{x^3 + 3}, \quad \text{picked by one stored parity bit}$$

</div>

Store $x$ and one bit for whether $y$ is even or odd. To rebuild the point, compute $x^3 + 3$, take its square root — which gives both $y$ and $-y$ — and use the bit to pick the right one. If $x^3 + 3$ isn't a square at all, no point exists with that $x$. This is exactly how a compressed public key fits a 256-bit $x$ plus one bit instead of a full 512-bit pair.

</MathBox>

## Turning a number into a point

The reverse direction matters too: given an arbitrary number — say, the output of a hash — can it be turned into a point on the curve?

<Figure caption="Fig. 7 — mapping a number onto the curve by trying it as x: x = 2 fails the square test, so the search increments to x = 3, which works, giving the point (3, 2).">
  <HashToCurveFigure />
</Figure>

<MathBox label="A basic hash-to-curve">

<div slot="formula">

$$\text{try } x, x+1, x+2, \ldots \text{ until } x^3 + 3 \text{ is a square}$$

</div>

Try a candidate $x$, test whether $x^3 + 3$ is a square, and increment if it isn't. Because exactly half of all numbers are squares — the fact this lesson opened with — about half of all $x$ values work, so the odds of failing $n$ times in a row fall off as $1/2^n$ and a few tries are always enough. Real systems use more careful versions of this, since the number of tries taken can leak timing information.

</MathBox>

## Where these tools show up

Three tools, all built from the same idea: telling squares apart from non-squares, and moving between a number and its root.

<Figure caption="Fig. 8 — the square test, square roots, and compression/hash-to-curve, and what each one is for.">
  <QuadResSummaryFigure />
</Figure>

The square test gives a fast yes-or-no on whether a root exists at all. Square roots — the easy shortcut or Tonelli–Shanks — actually solve $y^2 = a$. And compression plus hash-to-curve are where both get used in practice: shrinking a stored point down to $x$ and one bit, and turning arbitrary data into a point on the curve. But every one of these tools has a gap: $2$ has no square root mod $13$ at all. The next lesson builds a bigger field that contains one.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Exactly half of the nonzero numbers mod a prime are squares (quadratic residues), because $x$ and $-x$ always square to the same value.
- A square is exactly an even power of a generator, since squaring any power doubles its exponent — this is why the split is exactly half and half.
- The square test — raising $a$ to $(p-1)/2$ — answers "is $a$ a square?" in one exponentiation; this quantity is the Legendre symbol.
- When $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, a square root is a single power, $a^{(p+1)/4}$; otherwise the Tonelli–Shanks algorithm finds it by fixing a guess until its error term reaches $1$.
- Point compression stores a curve point as $x$ plus one parity bit and rebuilds $y$ with a square root; hash-to-curve runs the idea in reverse, trying values of $x$ until $x^3 + 3$ is a square.

</KeyTakeaways>
